Brussels transit: what drives the wait, and can we predict it?¶

Data: the Brussels Lakehouse (Azure ADLS Gen2). STIB live waiting times every 5 minutes, air quality (IRCEL) and Belgian grid load (Elia) every 15 minutes, cleaned by dbt into two gold tables:

  • stop_line_hourly: one row per stop × line × hour, with excess_wait_min = how many minutes longer people wait than the timetable promises.
  • city_hourly: one row per hour for the whole city: excess wait, NO₂, PM10, PM2.5, grid load.

Questions

  1. Is the data complete and clean?
  2. Does the wait move with air quality or electricity load, or is that just rush hour?
  3. Can a model predict next hour's excess wait at each stop better than simple rules? Baselines vs LightGBM vs a neural network.

This notebook is the report, not the computation. With five weeks of data (1.7 M stop-line-hours) doing everything in one Jupyter kernel ran out of memory, so the heavy work is split into steps that bash run.sh transit_delay runs first (settings in tasks/transit_delay/config.yaml):

Step What Output
features.py + features.sql DuckDB builds the model table straight from the Parquet files data/transit_delay/features.parquet, per_day.csv
train.py --nn baselines, LightGBM, neural network on the standard split; MLflow + model registry outputs/transit_delay/test_predictions.parquet, models/
evaluate.py test MAE of every model, result figures metrics.csv, figures/07..10
backtest.py the same models on each of the last weeks in turn backtest.csv, backtest.md

This notebook only reads those files (about 150 MB of memory) and explains them.

Cleaning rules (in features.sql)

  • Days with fewer than 20 hours of data are partial (the collector starting up, or today): left out.
  • A disrupted day is one whose median excess wait is more than 3× the normal median. It is real data, so it is not deleted, but it is left out of training (a model should learn normal service) and reported apart.
  • Excess waits beyond ±30 min are prediction glitches in STIB's feed (a vehicle cancelled, then reappearing); they are clipped so one glitch cannot dominate the error.

3. Air quality, electricity and the wait: correlation¶

The trap: rush hour raises traffic, NO₂, power use and transit delays at the same time. A raw correlation mostly measures that shared daily rhythm. So each measure is also correlated within the hour of day: subtract that hour's average first, and ask "when NO₂ is higher than usual for 08:00, is the wait also higher than usual for 08:00?" That is the number that could mean a real link. Even then: correlation is not causation.

4. The prediction task¶

Predict next hour's excess wait for every stop × line, using only what is known an hour before.

Feature Why it is allowed
stop, line, mode, hour of day, weekday known in advance
scheduled wait published timetable
excess wait of this stop/line one hour earlier already measured
average excess wait of this stop/line/hour-of-day on earlier days only no future data inside
city excess wait, NO₂, grid load one hour earlier already measured

Not allowed: actual_wait_min (it is the answer) and snapshots (only known once the hour is over). The features are computed in SQL (features.sql); a test checks they equal the original pandas code on the 18-day snapshot and that changing later data never changes an earlier row's features.

Split by time, never at random: a random split puts Thursday 08:05 in training and 08:10 in the test, so the model "remembers" instead of predicting. Training = the first days, test = the last quarter of the days, the two days before the test = validation (early stopping).

5. Baselines: the numbers any model has to beat¶

Error = MAE, the average of |predicted − actual| in minutes. Easy to explain, not dominated by glitches.

  • Always 0: trust the timetable.
  • Average per stop/line/hour of day: the mean excess wait of that stop, line and hour over the training days.
  • Same as one hour ago: this stop/line's excess wait in the previous hour.

6. LightGBM (gradient-boosted trees)¶

Builds hundreds of small decision trees, each correcting the errors of the ones before. Handles categories (stop, line) and missing values natively; usually the strongest model on tables like this. Fitted twice: with all features, and without the air/grid/city ones, to see whether those help.

7. A neural network¶

A multilayer perceptron with embeddings: each stop, line, hour and weekday gets a small learned vector (like word embeddings in NLP), concatenated with the numeric features and passed through two hidden layers. Trained with the L1 loss (= MAE) and early stopping on the validation days. CPU is enough (train.py --nn).

9b. Is one test period enough? Rolling backtest¶

One split can be lucky. backtest.py repeats the comparison on each recent week: train on everything before it, validate on the two days before it, test on the week.

LightGBM vs the best simple rule of each fold:

  • fold 1: 0.893 vs 0.970 (Average per stop/line/hour of day): 8.0% better
  • fold 2: 0.885 vs 0.970 (Average per stop/line/hour of day): 8.7% better

Mean gain 8.3% (min 8.0%, max 8.7%) over 2 folds.

Reading it. LightGBM beat every simple rule in all 2 folds; its edge ranges from 8.0% to 8.7%. With 2 folds, the ± is a rough spread, not a confidence interval. Later folds train on more days, so part of a difference between folds is more history, part is the week itself (holidays, disruptions). The disrupted-day flag is decided once on all the data (median rule), so it is not a forecast of disruptions; disrupted days stay in the test weeks and are scored like any other day.

10. What we found (run of 15 Nov 2026, data 10 Oct to 14 Nov)¶

Data. 1,729,314 stop-line-hours, 1,894 stops, 89 lines, no missing values. 35 full days (Fri 16 Oct and today, Sun 15 Nov, are partial and left out); one disrupted day (Mon 12 Oct: median excess wait 4.44 min against a normal 0.34 min), kept out of training. 361 glitch values clipped (0.02%). 1,659,005 rows in the model table.

Correlation. With five weeks, the links with the city measures are weaker than in the first 18 days: within the hour of day, vehicles on the road r = −0.14 (was −0.29), grid load +0.14 (+0.17), NO₂ +0.05 (+0.19). None of them is strong enough to matter for a prediction, and the model confirms it (below).

  • LightGBM still wins, but its edge over the best simple rule shrank from 11% to 8.5% (0.987 → 0.903). Most of the change is the rule getting better: with five weeks of history, "the average of this stop, line and hour" is a much better guess (1.146 → 0.987), and that average is also LightGBM's main input.
  • The rolling backtest agrees: on the two most recent weeks LightGBM is 8.0% and 8.7% better than the best rule (0.893 vs 0.970, 0.885 vs 0.970). Only two weeks have the 14 training days the backtest asks for, so the spread between folds says little.
  • Air quality, grid load and city delays still do not help: without them the model is slightly better, on the standard split and in both backtest weeks.
  • The model without city features used all 2,000 trees in every run (early stopping never triggered), so it is not fully converged; more trees or a higher learning rate may gain a little more.
  • The neural network is close (0.921) but trees still win on this table.
  • Hardest to predict: line 72 (3.2 min mean error) and the night buses (N04 to N18): few vehicles per hour, so one late bus moves the average a lot.
